AI & Tech Daily News, November 7, 2026

This week’s reports touch three very different parts of AI work: generating video, accelerating AI research, and judging messy identity matches. One is a product update with direct features and pricing. Another raises a difficult question about how much AI contributes to AI progress. The third tests a practical way to spend less on model decisions without giving up the stronger model’s judgment on hard cases.

Gemini Omni gets longer clips and lower pricing

Google announced Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash on November 5. The company says the video model can generate clips up to 15 seconds, compared with 10 seconds in Omni 1.1, and costs 30 percent less. The announced price is $0.07 per second for 720p video. Those are concrete changes, and they tell developers more than a vague promise of better generation would.

The workflow Google describes is familiar to anyone who has tried to get a generated clip right: make a quick draft, adjust a detail, then spend more on the version worth keeping. Omni 1.2 Flash supports conversational edits, so a user can ask for a change to an existing clip rather than regenerating everything. Google also describes a path from lower-resolution drafts to 1080p or 4K output. That could save time and cost when a small correction is all a clip needs, though the real value depends on how reliably edits preserve everything that was already working.

Reference consistency is the other claim worth watching. Google says the model can use images, short motion clips, and audio cues, while keeping characters, objects, and details consistent across a sequence. That is exactly where video generation can become frustrating. A character may look right in one frame and drift in the next. Longer output makes that problem more visible, not less. The announcement describes improvements, but teams should still test their own subjects and prompts before building a production workflow around them.

AI research may be speeding up AI research

A post at Planned Obsolescence examines a recent public METR report on AI research and development acceleration at Anthropic. The author says the report found capabilities progress at Anthropic had been about twice as fast from October 2025 to October 2026 as during the prior twelve months. The post argues that AI automation is probably responsible for most of the continued faster pace, while also noting that much of the supporting evidence in the report is redacted.

That distinction matters. The report, as discussed in the post, is not a clean experiment that isolates one cause. The author describes evidence and an interpretation, then draws a more alarming conclusion about what the rate could imply if the acceleration keeps compounding. Readers should keep those layers separate: a reported measurement, an explanation for it, and an extrapolation into the future are different claims with different levels of certainty.

Still, the question deserves attention. If researchers use AI systems to write code, run experiments, or sift through results, the tools may increase the rate at which new AI systems improve. But measuring the effect is hard. More computing power, better algorithms, staffing, and changes in research priorities can all affect progress at once. A report with redacted evidence leaves outsiders unable to check some of the reasoning directly. That does not make the findings useless. It does mean confident predictions should be treated with care.

Hightouch tests a cheaper judge with Opus as backup

Hightouch’s identity-resolution test looks at a narrower engineering problem: deciding whether different records refer to the same person. The company tested smaller decision models against frontier models, then tried a hybrid approach where a cheaper model handles ordinary cases and asks Claude Opus for help when its confidence calls for a second opinion. Hightouch reports that the hybrid Jev setup reached close to Opus-level accuracy at more than four times lower cost.

Identity matching is a useful test because real data is untidy. Names get misspelled, addresses change, fields are blank, and two people can share details. A model that confidently merges the wrong records can cause real trouble for analytics and customer outreach. The post says its decision models performed between 88.8 and 97.3 percent accuracy without a second opinion, depending on the model and test conditions. It also says the hybrid setup sends harder cases to Opus rather than paying frontier-model prices for every record.

That is a sensible design pattern, but the confidence gate is doing a lot of work. A cheap model that knows when it is unsure can hand off difficult decisions. One that is overconfident may keep bad matches to itself. Hightouch says Jev’s confidence was better calibrated than Luna’s in its tests, while Luna did better out of the box. Those results are specific to the company’s test data and setup, which used generated data. A team considering the approach should test its own error patterns and measure the cost of false matches, not only average accuracy.

Across these stories, the practical theme is restraint. Longer video output is useful only if edits stay consistent. A claim about faster AI research needs clear evidence and careful interpretation. A cheaper judge earns trust only when it knows which cases to escalate. The gains are real possibilities, but each depends on a condition that should be tested rather than assumed.
