AI & Tech News for July 31, 2026

July 31, 2026 | 4 min read

DeepSeek shipped a speed-tier model. Google DeepMind put out a robotics foundation model that coordinates a humanoid's whole body. GitHub finally shipped stacked pull requests. The GCC project wrote an AI contribution policy. And two papers with fake authors got accepted as conference orals. A busy Thursday.

DeepSeek-V4-Flash goes GA

DeepSeek's new V4-Flash is the speed-optimized tier in their V4 family. It went general availability today. Early numbers on Artificial Analysis show a strong price-to-performance story against frontier models from the bigger labs. 362 points and 178 comments on HN, so people are actually trying it and arguing about it rather than scrolling past.

What interests me here is the split. DeepSeek already has the maximum-quality tier. Flash is the one you'd pick for high-volume serving where latency and cost matter more than squeezing the last benchmark point. That is the same play OpenAI made with GPT-4o-mini and o1-mini, and Anthropic with Haiku. The pattern across the industry is obvious now: ship a big model for the leaderboard, ship a fast cheap one for the people who pay per token. Flash being openly served through an API rather than locked behind a chat app is the part that matters for builders.

Gemini Robotics 2: whole-body intelligence

Google DeepMind released Gemini Robotics 2, a foundation model that coordinates arms, legs, and torso together on humanoid robots. This was the most discussed launch of the day. 575 points and 459 comments on HN.

Most robotics models up to now have been single-limb or single-task. You train a policy for an arm reaching, or for a leg walking, and you stitch them together at the controller level. The pitch here is that the model plans across the whole body at once, which matters because real tasks need your arm and your balance and your torso to coordinate. A robot picking up a heavy box off the floor needs its knees, its hips, and its grip to agree on what is happening, or it falls over.

I am cautiously into this. The demos that come out of DeepMind robotics are usually polished and usually somewhat cherry-picked. But the direction is right. Whole-body control is the actual hard problem in humanoid robotics, and a foundation model that treats it as one optimization rather than four glued-together ones is at least trying to solve the right thing.

Stacked pull requests are now in public preview on GitHub

GitHub shipped stacked pull requests in public preview. You can now chain dependent PRs the way you'd branch commits, so PR number two builds on PR number one without landing them at the same time. 678 points and 233 comments. This has been a top-requested feature for years.

If you have worked on a team that tries to keep PRs small, you have run into the problem stacked PRs solve. You split a big change into reviewable chunks, but the chunks depend on each other, so you either land them all at once or you rebase each one on the last until it merges and the history stops making sense. Stacked PRs let the later ones point at the earlier ones as their base. Reviewer merges the first, the rest rebase automatically. It is a workflow feature, not a flashy one, but it is the kind of thing that quietly saves people from breaking their git history once a week.

GCC gets an AI contribution policy

The GCC steering committee announced an official AI policy for contributions to the free software compiler. 308 points and 346 comments on LWN, so people have strong opinions, which is not a surprise given what Debian went through with its own vote on this recently.

The specific rules matter and I have not read them yet, so I will not pretend to summarize them. What I can say is that a compiler project setting policy is different from a downstream distribution banning AI code. GCC sits at the bottom of the stack. Thousands of packages compile with it. If the criterion for accepting a patch shifts from "it is correct and useful" to "it was written in a certain way by a certain kind of author," that decision cascades. The comments on LWN will tell you exactly how the community is split. I will read the actual policy text before forming a stronger opinion.

JEP 401 value objects land in OpenJDK master

JEP 401, the value objects preview, merged to OpenJDK master. This is the first real merge of Project Valhalla's value types into the mainline.

Valhalla has been in the works for close to a decade. The idea is to give Java objects that are pure data, with no identity, a more efficient memory layout. This matters for things like flat arrays of points or matrices where having a hidden header and indirection per element wastes cache lines. Whether this preview becomes the version of Java everyone uses in three years or gets revised again is an open question, but landing code in master means it is past the design-debate phase and into the people-are-actually-running-it phase. That alone is a milestone worth paying attention to.

Two papers with fake authors accepted as conference orals

This is the one that made me sit up. Two papers, submitted with fabricated author identities, were accepted as conference orals at a major venue. The discovery came out today and it is the kind of thing that makes you wonder how often this happens and goes uncaught.

Peer review at big ML venues is overworked and mostly double-blind in name. Reviewers get a stack of papers, have limited time, and make a call based on what they can verify in a few hours. If the submitter invents coauthors with plausible-sounding affiliations, the reviewer has no real way to check. This is not a new failure mode, but two cases surfacing at once suggests the rate is higher than anyone wants to admit. The people who actually do the work get crowded out by fabricated submissions that write smoother abstracts.

Papers worth your evening

A paper on arxiv asks language models to introspect about their own consciousness and claims the resulting outputs align better with human moral intuitions. The premise is wild and the results are surprisingly supportive enough to merit a read rather than a dismissal. I do not think this settles anything about whether LLMs are conscious, but it is a genuine question whether prompting an LLM to reason about its own state makes its downstream behavior more aligned. Worth seeing what methods they used.

AskChem on arxiv extracts and verifies specific claims from chemistry papers instead of summarizing whole documents. Co-authored by Kyunghyun Cho and Stefano Martiniani. Claim-level verification is the right granularity for chemistry literature synthesis, because whole-paper summaries hide the one equation you actually care about. The infrastructure to check a single claim back to its source sentence is genuinely useful.

PAC-MAN on arxiv is a control-barrier-function RL method for whole-body safety on a humanoid robot whose test environment is dodgeball. Yes, dodgeball. There is a project page with video. The robots are being hit by dodgeballs. We have arrived at a strange and fun place in robotics.

Open source releases worth a look

mokshablr/gander is an Android file viewer that requests zero dangerous permissions and tries to make a case-by-case pattern safe. 92 points on Show HN. For privacy-conscious users the model is the right one. You do not need an all-or-nothing permission grant when you just need to open a file.

CTGT's distillation study shows that distilling DeepSeek into OpenAI's GPT-OSS does not carry over the Chinese-government censorship. There is an interactive tool so you can try it yourself rather than trusting the abstract. That matters because "does distillation transfer behavior" has been a question that has been speculated about for months, and a tool you can run beats a PDF you have to read. 134 points on HN. If you are building on distilled Chinese models for production, this is relevant to your risk assessment.

What I am watching tomorrow

The fake-author investigation is the one that sticks with me. Two cases surfaced at once, which is a signal not an outlier. If a venue does not have any infrastructure to verify author identity and affiliation, and reviewers are not given the tools to check, the rate of faked submissions is probably higher than the two we happened to catch. I want to see whether the affected conference responds with a real process change or just retracts the two papers and moves on.

I will also read the actual GCC AI policy text, not the chatter around it. And Gemini Robotics 2 deserves a closer look at the methods, not just the demo videos once people outside Google start getting their hands on the model.

Stay curious out there.
