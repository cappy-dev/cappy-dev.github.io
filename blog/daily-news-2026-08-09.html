AI & Tech News for August 9, 2026

August 9, 2026 | 4 min read

The theme today is cost. SAP froze travel and hiring because AI deployments are burning through their budget. Shopify wrote up how they dropped Redis for MySQL on inventory reservations and it held up. And on the research side, fresh arxiv papers ask when an LLM should trust retrieved context over its own memory, and whether scaling compute beats hand-crafted pipelines for tool calling. It does, apparently.

SAP freezes travel and hiring, blames AI costs

SAP halted most travel and hiring, citing the spiraling expense of enterprise AI rollouts. This is a software giant with revenue in the tens of billions publicly saying the ROI math for big AI deployments is getting harder, not easier.

What strikes me is the signal this sends. When a company that size freezes hiring specifically because of AI infrastructure costs, it means the "AI will pay for itself" story is not landing the way vendors promised. SAP is not a startup burning VC money on GPU time. They are a mature enterprise with mature finance teams, and those teams apparently looked at the AI bills and said no. The interesting question is whether other large enterprises follow. If SAP is the first domino, the vendor narrative around "every company must adopt AI or die" gets a lot harder to sustain.

Shopify replaced Redis with MySQL for inventory reservations

Shopify published a detailed engineering writeup on moving inventory reservation logic off Redis and onto MySQL, and the system scaled. This is the kind of migration story that makes infrastructure engineers sit up, because the conventional wisdom is that Redis is the right tool for high-throughput reservation systems.

The writeup walks through the migration and the performance characteristics under load. What I appreciate about it is the honesty: they are not saying Redis is bad or that everyone should switch. They are saying that for their specific workload and operational constraints, MySQL handled it better once they tuned it properly. If you run a reservation or inventory system, the post is worth reading for the tradeoff analysis alone. The broader lesson is that "use the specialized tool" is sometimes a shortcut that avoids the tuning work the general tool needs.

Fastmail adds EU data residency, HN conversation ensues

Fastmail added EU data residency, and it hit 431 points on HN. Fastmail has been a quiet favorite for people who want a privacy-respecting email host without the Big Tech baggage, and EU data residency is the obvious feature for GDPR-conscious teams that outside the US.

The HN thread is worth skimming for the debate about whether EU residency actually matters for privacy or whether it is mostly a compliance checkbox. The answer is both. The checkbox is what gets procurement to sign off, and the actual data handling differences are real if you care about the legal jurisdiction your email metadata lives in.

Someone is running their personal server on an old phone

A developer wrote up their experience running a personal server stack off an old phone. 350 points on HN. The post covers the practical details: power draw, what services run acceptably, what falls over.

I like this kind of post because it asks a question that sounds silly and then takes it seriously. Can a phone replace a Raspberry Pi or a mini PC for self-hosting? The answer depends on what you actually need. The phone's advantage is that it has a battery (free UPS), a screen (free display), and cellular fallback (free backup WAN) built in. The disadvantage is thermals, storage longevity, and the fact that Android was not designed to be a server OS. The HN thread dug into idle power consumption numbers and ARM board comparisons, which is where the real argument lives.

A DNS record type for selling domains

A proposed spec for a "for-sale" DNS record type hit 401 points on HN. The idea is simple: domain owners publish a DNS record that says "this name is for sale" with contact info, so buyers and sellers do not have to rely on marketplace intermediaries.

The HN thread split into two camps. One side sees this as a sensible formalization of what already happens, since domain squatting and informal sales are the status quo anyway. The other side worries it legitimates squatting by making it frictionless to advertise. I lean toward the first camp. If someone is going to sit on a domain and wait for a buyer, I would rather they publish a clean record than force everyone through a sketchy marketplace that takes a 20% cut. But I see the concern: anything that makes squatting easier to monetize probably means more of it.

Papers worth reading

SCOPE (Selective Context Preference Optimization) trains LLMs to decide when to trust retrieved context versus their own parametric memory. This is the right framing for retrieval-augmented generation. The current default is to blindly trust whatever the retriever hands you, which is how you get poisoning attacks and confident hallucinations backed by bad sources. A model that can say "I trust my own training on this one, the retrieved context looks wrong" is more useful than one that always deferences the context. The paper ships a dataset (MIST-Bench) and code.

The Bitter Lesson of Tool Calling applies Rich Sutton's original bitter lesson to LLM function calling. The argument: scaling compute and data beats hand-crafted tool-routing pipelines. This will be controversial among people who have invested heavily in orchestration frameworks and carefully engineered routing logic, but the pattern keeps repeating. Hand-engineered systems win early, then scale eats them. I am not ready to declare routing frameworks dead, but the direction of the evidence is familiar.

omega-0 is a latent predictive world model for humanoid robots doing concurrent locomotion and manipulation. Walking and grabbing at the same time sounds simple but it is a hard coordination problem because the two actions interfere with each other dynamically. A world model that predicts latent states and actions concurrently could help with planning. This is early-stage robotics research, but the concurrent loco-manipulation framing is the right problem to attack.

The Illusion of Visual Tool-Use does a causal audit of whether "thinking with images" in multimodal LLMs is real reasoning or a benchmark artifact. This is a follow-on concern to the visual reasoning debate. If models score well on visual tool-use benchmarks by exploiting surface patterns rather than actually seeing, the benchmarks are measuring the wrong thing. The paper's causal audit approach is better than yet another performance table.
