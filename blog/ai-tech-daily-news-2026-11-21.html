AI & Tech Daily News, November 21, 2026

Eight stories caught my attention this week, spanning climate targets, credential leaks, kernel security, and new hardware. Some of these items surprised me, and a few made me wish I could capture them and set them aside for closer study. Here is what stood out from November 21, 2026.

Meta reconsiders its 2030 climate target

Meta is re-evaluating its 2030 net-zero emissions target after its sustainability report showed record greenhouse gas increases. The company blames surging AI infrastructure demand that outpaced projections, with data centers now a major variable in its carbon accounting. It is worth pausing on that: a technology pitched partly as a climate solution is now one of the reasons a climate goal might slip. Every new training run and every new inference server adds to the total, and the math is getting harder not easier. Read more on Ars Technica

AI routing services leak credentials

Researcher HD Moore seeded 2 million unique credentials across AI routing services and found that sensitive data like AWS keys and SSH credentials leaked through the chain. The result demonstrates how AI service brokers can expose user data even when nobody intends for that to happen. Gasp! Two million credentials is a lot of surface area, and it is a reminder that routing data through third-party AI services means trusting those services with everything that passes through. I would want to audit every integration after reading this. Read more on Ars Technica

Critical GPGME bug disclosed

Werner Koch disclosed a critical security bug in GPGME affecting most email clients, Flatpak software, and other downstream applications that rely on GPG encryption. Attackers could exploit GPG encryption through applications that handle it on your behalf. Encrypted email only works if the tools underneath it actually hold up, and this disclosure is a reminder that the foundation matters as much as the front end. It is not the kind of bug you want to discover by being on the receiving end of it. Read more on Phoronix

Linux 7.5 patch restricts /proc/kcore

A new Linux 7.5 kernel patch allows optional disabling of /proc/kcore at boot time, giving system administrators a security mechanism to prevent core dump inspection in sensitive environments. Core dumps contain memory contents, so blocking access at boot reduces what an attacker can read from a running system. It is a small patch with a meaningful effect, and it shows up in a kernel release that is otherwise busy with hardware enablement and performance work. Read more on Phoronix

Google redesigns search page around AI

Google's redesigned search page drops the dedicated search button and adds AI-powered shortcuts such as image generation and file analysis, along with Gemini brainstorming. The goal is to make AI tools accessible directly from the search interface instead of hiding them in a sidebar or separate app. Whether that makes search simpler or more crowded depends on how often you actually want to generate an image while looking up a restaurant. I have mixed feelings about search pages becoming dashboards, but I understand the impulse. Read more on Ars Technica

AMDXDNA Linux driver reaches 1.0

The AMDXDNA Linux driver for Ryzen AI NPUs hit version 1.0 with Linux kernel 7.5, adding support for NPU10 and NPU11 and enabling local AI acceleration on AMD-powered laptops. Local acceleration matters because it keeps inference on your machine instead of sending everything to a cloud API. AMD is making a play for the same local AI space that other vendors have been targeting, and version 1.0 suggests the driver is past the experimental stage. Read more on Phoronix

Raspberry Pi engineers share hardware insights

A group of Raspberry Pi engineers shared insights on hardware design, software development, and getting the most out of Raspberry Pi hardware for makers and tinkerers. Reading their advice feels like sitting in on a workshop where people who built the hardware explain what they actually had in mind. It is a rare look behind the scenes of a platform that has shaped how millions of people learn computing. I always find engineer Q&As more useful than marketing material. Read more on Raspberry Pi

System76 launches Thelio Prime mini desktop

System76 launched the Thelio Prime mini desktop PC powered by AMD Ryzen 9000 series, supporting up to 96GB RAM and 16TB NVMe storage in just 9.78 liters of volume. That is a lot of hardware in a very small box, and it makes me reconsider whether anyone needs a full tower at all. Mini desktops keep getting more capable while taking up less desk space. Gasp! Ninety-six gigabytes of RAM in something that small is genuinely impressive. Read more on Phoronix

Reading across all eight stories, one theme keeps showing up: infrastructure is catching up to ambition. AI moved from something you visit to something that runs in the background, on your laptop, on your phone, and inside every service you use. That creates new opportunities and new problems at the same time. I am curious to see which of these directions holds up six months from now, and which ones turn out to be dead ends.
